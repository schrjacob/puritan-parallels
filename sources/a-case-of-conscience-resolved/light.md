%% section: epistle
%% number: 
%% title: The Epistle Dedicatory

It is far from me to despise you, or to do anything to your reproach. I know you are beloved of God for the sake of Christ, and that you stand fixed forever by faith upon the same foundation with us. I also know that the Lord makes no difference between male and female as to the communication of his saving graces, but has often made many of your sex eminent for piety — indeed, there have been, among ordinary Christians, women who for holiness of life have outdone many of the brethren. Their virtuous lives can only bring renown and glory to you, and conviction to those of us who have fallen behind you in faith and holiness. The love of women in spiritual things, as in natural things, often outdoes that of men.

When Christ was on earth, we do not read that any man did as much for him as did the woman who was a sinner, or Joanna, Susanna, and many others (Luke 7:36–38, chapter 8:1–3). And as they have shown themselves eminent for piety, so also for Christian courage and strength of mind when called by God to bear witness to his name in the world, as all histories of that kind sufficiently testify. It was women, I take it, as well as men, who were tortured and would not accept release, that they might obtain a better resurrection (Heb. 11:35). Wherefore I honor and praise your eminence in virtue, and desire to be spurred on by the exceeding piety of any of you, in all holy conduct and godliness.

And although, as you will find, I have not without cause questioned the lawfulness of your assembling together by yourselves to perform solemn worship to God without your men, yet I dare not make you the authors of your own error in this. I rather impute it to your leaders, who — whether from a fond regard for some seeming ability they think is in you for this, or from a persuasion that you have been better than themselves in other things, or from a misguided zeal — have put upon you a work far too heavy for you. I will not at this time trouble myself to inquire which of these it is. But this is certain, at least as I see it: in this matter you have been tempted by them to take too much upon yourselves.

I am well aware that by writing this, although I intend your honor and good order by it, I am likely enough to run the gauntlet among you, and to feel sharply the scourge of some tongues, and to be soundly browbeaten for it by others — especially by our author, who will find himself immediately concerned, since I have blamed him for what he has irregularly done, both with the Word, to you, and to me. I expect also to be sufficiently slandered, and counted a man against prayer, and meetings for prayer, and the like; but I will labor to bear these things with patience, and to seek the good of those who are tempted to abuse me for it.

I would not, indeed I would not, have spoken a word to this question in this manner, had not Mr. K. sent his paper abroad and among us, encouraging this practice among us, in opposition to our peace. I do not say he intended our breach, but his arguments tended toward it; and had our people not been of a wise and quiet temper, his paper might have set us into a flame. But thanks be to God we are at peace, and walk in love, notwithstanding the efforts that have been made to stir us to do otherwise. There are also some whose mouths have opened against me for this, who lie in wait for occasions, and show that they are glad to seize them before they are given by me — to whom I now show, by this ensuing discourse, that I had reason to do what I did.

I commend you to God, and to the word of his grace, which is able to build you up, and to give you an inheritance among those who are sanctified by faith in Jesus Christ, to whom be honor and glory forever. And I remain your faithful friend and brother, to pray for you, to love you in the Gospel, and to do you what Christian service I can. John Bunyan.

%% section: occasion
%% number: 
%% title: How This Controversy Began

The occasion of my involvement in this controversy in this manner is this: after I had, for reasons best known to myself, searched and found that the so-called women's meetings lacked support in the Word, I called them into question within our fellowship. Having done so, my reasons for doing so were, quite reasonably, demanded of me, and I gave them, which caused that practice among us to cease. So subject to the Word were our women, and so willing to let go of what could not by it be proved a duty for them to practice. But after I had done this, by what means I do not know, Mr. K., hearing of my proceeding in this matter — though I think he knew little of the question or answer — set pen to paper and drew up four arguments to justify those meetings. These, once written, were sent down into our parts, not to me, but to some of his own persuasion, who kept them, or sent them, or lent them out as they thought fit. And so, about two years later, with the note immediately following, they were conveyed to my hand.

Brother Bunyan, this enclosed was sent to me from godly women, whose custom for a long time has been to meet together to pray, who, hearing of your contrary opinion, sent this. It came from Mr. K., who would like to know what objections you have against it, and he is ready to give his further advice. Please be pleased to give your answer in writing, for Mr. K. expects it. Please be pleased to leave your answer with S. F. in Bedford. Your friends in the Lord, S. B., S. F.

Now, having received the papers and considered their contents, I was at first uncertain within myself whether the thing was fabricated or true, and for that purpose wrote to these women again. But recalling that I had heard something of this before, I concluded there was ground to believe it as I do, and so resolved to answer his demand and expectation. But to say nothing more on this, I will next present you with the arguments he sent, and then with my manner of handling them.

%% section: mrk-arguments
%% number: 
%% title: Mr. K.'s Arguments for Women's Prayer Meetings

First he begins with this question: whether women fearing God may meet to pray together, and whether it be lawful for them to do so. Having done that, he falls to wondering, saying it seems very strange to him that any who profess the fear of God can question the lawfulness of it, since the rule for praying is so general to all, and there are so many instances of the practice, on several occasions, in the Word of God, for their encouragement in it.

First, he says, if women may praise God together for mercies received, for the church of God or for themselves, then they may pray together — the proof of which is plain in Exodus 15:20–21. If it be objected that the case was extraordinary, and that Miriam was a prophetess, I answer that the danger of ruin and destruction, and our deliverance from it, if the Lord grants it, cannot be regarded as anything but extraordinary, the designs of ruin against the church and servants of God being as great now as when God delivered his people from Pharaoh's hand — and this will call for praise, if the Lord sends deliverance, just as then. And whereas it is further objected that Miriam was an extraordinary person, I answer that the duty itself of praising God for the mercy was incumbent on all, inasmuch as they were all partakers of the mercy; and the same Spirit of Christ that was in her is also in all his servants, given for the same end, both to pray for mercies we need and to praise God for them.

Secondly, if women, in eminent danger to themselves and the church of God, have prayed jointly together for deliverance, and God has answered and approved of it, then women may jointly pray together. The instance we have is famous: Esther 4:16. There we see she and her maidens prayed and fasted together, and the Lord gave a gracious answer and deliverance.

Thirdly, if God has, in Gospel times, promised the pouring out of his Spirit to women, to that very end that they may pray together apart from men, then it is not only their liberty but their duty to meet and pray together. But God has promised his Spirit to that end (Zech. 12:10–13), which Scripture, it is plain, is a promise of Gospel times. And it is to be noted that the text does not say, in the singular, HE shall pray apart, and HIS wife apart, but THEY shall pray apart, and THEIR wives apart. And Malachi 3:16: God takes notice of all who speak often together, and call upon his name.

Fourthly, if God has so approved of women meeting together to pray in Gospel times, that he even used the occasion to make known to them his mind and will concerning Jesus Christ, then it is lawful for women to pray together. But God has so approved of their meeting to pray together (Acts 16:13), by which text it appears it was a frequent practice for women to meet and pray together.

These are Mr. K.'s arguments; the conclusion of his paper follows. And besides all these particular instances, says he, what do those general rules mean, to build up one another in our most holy faith, and pray in the Holy Ghost (Jude 20)? It extends to all who believe, both men and women, unless any will say women are not to be built up in their most holy faith. Therefore let no one hinder you from a duty so incumbent upon you in a special manner, in such a day as this. Cannot many women who have used this practice, by experience, say they have met with the Lord in it, and have found many blessed returns of prayer from God, both to themselves and the church, wherein God has owned them? Therefore, what God has borne witness to and approved of, let no man deter you from. Please turn to the Scriptures quoted, which I hope will give you full satisfaction.

%% section: answer-opening
%% number: 
%% title: Bunyan's Opening Remarks

These are his arguments, and this his conclusion, in which I cannot but say there is not only boldness but flattery — boldness, in fathering his misunderstanding upon the authority of the Word of God; and flattery, in soothing persons in a way of their own choosing, by making them judges in their own cause. Which I hope to make further appear presently.

Since his women, in their letter, told me that Mr. K. expects my answer, I count myself called to show the unsoundness of his opinion. He would, as they suggest, confine me to answering in writing; but his papers have circulated I know not where, and I know no other way to check his extravagances except by scattering my own abroad. And as I will not be confined to a written answer, neither will I be confined to his methods of argument. What sort of scholar he is, I do not know; for my part, I am not ashamed to confess that I know neither the mode nor figure of a syllogism, nor scarcely which is major or minor. I perceive but little sense, and far less truth, in his arguments; and I hold that he has stretched and strained the Holy Word out of place, to make it, were it possible, shore up his fond conceits. I shall therefore first take these texts away from the errors to which he has joined them, and then fall to picking the bones of his syllogisms.

But as I will not confine myself to his mode and way of arguing, neither will I take up his question as he states the matter in controversy. Instead I will propound the same question here which, in substance, was handled among us when the thing itself was in doubt: whether, where a church of Christ is situated, it is the duty of the women of that congregation, ordinarily and by appointment, to separate themselves from their brethren, and so to assemble together, to perform some parts of divine worship, such as prayer, without their men? This was our question, this we debated, and Mr. K. might have sent for it and spoken to it, since he will insist on being a confuter.

And courteous reader, since I have presented you with the question, I will also present you with the method I took when I handled it among my brethren: first, I opened the terms of the question; second, I showed what assemblies were used to perform divine worship to God; third, I showed whose prayers in such worship were used, or desired by Paul and others.

%% section: stating-question
%% number: 
%% title: Stating the Question

For the first: by "church of Christ" I mean one gathered or constituted by, and walking after, the rule of the Word of God. By "situated" I mean wherever such a church happens to be, whether as a whole or in its parts. By "separating" I mean their meeting together by their own appointment, and, so met, attempting to perform divine worship — prayer — without their men.

Having thus explained the question, as a preparation toward answering it, I come next to show what kind of assemblies were used of old to perform divine worship to God. I find there have been three sorts of assemblies in which divine worship has been performed. First, it has been performed in mixed assemblies made up of saints and sinners together, in which the saints were edified and sinners convinced, converted, and brought to confess their sins, to the glory of God — of these assemblies we read in Matthew 5, 13, and 23, Mark 4, 2, 6, and 10, Luke 5, 8, 12, 13, 15, and 20, 1 Corinthians 14:24, and many other Scriptures. Second, I also find that the church by herself, distinct from the world, has met together to perform worship on its own (Mark 4:34, Acts 12:4, 13:1–2, 15:4, 20:7–8, John 20:19, 26). Third, I find also that assemblies for divine worship have been made up of the elders and principal brethren of the church alone, with none of the rest of the congregation present (Matt. 10:1, Luke 9:1, Acts 1:3, 2:17–18, Gal. 2:1–2, with several other Scriptures besides).

But in all Scripture I find not that the women of the churches of Christ were accustomed to separate themselves from their brethren, and, as so separated, perform worship together among themselves, or within their congregation; nor that they made, with the allowance of the Word, any appointment so to do. Thus far, therefore, this must stand as a human invention, and Mr. K. as the promoter of it.

This done, in the third place, I come to show you whose prayers, or by whose mouth prayer in such lawful assemblies used to be made, or by Paul or others desired. First, whose prayers were used, or who was the mouth: I find them called the prayers of the church in general, or of the principal men thereof in particular (Judg. 2:4–5, 20:8, 26, Joel 1:14, 2:15–16, Acts 12:5, 13:1, 3). Second, when Paul or others desired that prayers should be made for them by others, they desired the prayers of the church in general, or of the brethren in particular — never a women's meeting — either the prayers of the church in general (Col. 4:2, Phil. 1:9, 4:6, 1 Thess. 5:17, Heb. 13:18), or, if of certain persons, he calls only upon the men and brethren in particular, never upon a woman by name or sex (1 Thess. 5:25, 2 Thess. 3:1, Rom. 15:30, 1 Tim. 2:8). Nor was the apostle alone in this: Christ speaks a parable to the effect that men ought always to pray; James speaks of the effectual, fervent prayer of a righteous man; Moses sent the young men to sacrifice; and the people in Zechariah's time sent their men to pray before the Lord (Luke 18:1, James 5:16, Exod. 24:5, Zech. 7:2). I do not believe any of this despises the prayers of women, but by it we are taught who is commended to us as the mouth in assemblies for prayer.

One word more: the women in the time of the prophet Jeremiah, when they had made their cakes to the queen of heaven — though what they did seemed as right in their own eyes as if they had performed true worship indeed — when questioned by the prophet for what they had done, could not justify the act except by pleading that they had not done it without their men (Jer. 44:17–19).

Having premised these few things, I shall now come more directly to discourse of the question itself: whether, where a church of Christ is situated, it is the duty of the women of that congregation, ordinarily and by appointment, to separate themselves from their brethren, and, as so separated, to assemble together to perform divine worship — prayer — without their men? This was our question, and I will now give it a negative answer. For I find in Christ's testament neither command nor example for doing so; and where there is neither, it cannot be a duty upon them, nor even a liberty, but presumption to attempt it.

The command, says Mr. K., is general to all. But I answer: yet it is limited and confined as to order and manner of performance. Women may, indeed ought, to pray — what then? Is it their duty to help carry on prayer in public assemblies alongside men, to be the audible mouth there, before all, to God? No, certainly not — and yet the command is general to all to pray. Women of the churches of Christ have no command to separate themselves from the men of their congregations to perform prayer in their own company without them, and yet the command is general to all to pray. We must therefore distinguish persons and performances, though we may not exclude either. The manner and order in which such duties must be done, Mr. K. knows, is in some cases as essential as the very matter of worship.

%% section: five-reasons
%% number: 
%% title: Five Reasons for Dissenting

First, to appoint meetings for divine worship, whether of the whole church or of its parts, is an act of power — which power resides in the elders in particular, or in the church in general, but never in the women considered by themselves. Mr. K. does insinuate that this power resides in them too, for he says God has, in Gospel times, promised the Spirit to women to the very end that they may pray together apart from men. But if the Spirit is given them to that very end, then they have a power residing in themselves to call their own sex together to do it — and even a blind man could see where that conclusion leads. Meanwhile, our own claim is this: that calling the church, or parts of it, together to perform divine worship is an act of power residing in the church in general or the elders in particular. Under the Old Testament it was Moses, Aaron, and the priests who called the church together for worship; later it was the elders and chief men (Josh. 24:1, Ezra 10:5–9, Acts 14:26–27, 15:3). Where it was not the elders alone, it was still the power residing in the church, jointly exercised (Judg. 20:8, 18, Ezra 3:1, Zeph. 2:1–3, Acts 12:12, 1 Cor. 5:4, 11:20). But never, as far as I have read in the Bible, did ordinary believing women assume this power of the elders or the church to themselves. As for the meeting mentioned in Acts 12:12, it was appointed by the power of the church itself, meeting in several places for convenience, as a large persecuted congregation might. Nor do I question the lawfulness of a part of the church assembling for prayer, elders absent, so long as the men who call such meetings are countenanced by the elders or church to do it (1 Tim. 2:8, 2 Tim. 2:22). But that the sisters of a church may call their own sex together to perform such worship by themselves, I find no warrant for.

Secondly, because this kind of worship, when done in and by a company, is ministerial to that company as well as petitionary to God. That is, those who as the mouth in assemblies pray to God also teach that assembly, as well as beg mercies of him. And I do not find that women may assemble to do this. That such prayer is a kind of ministering in the Word to those standing by, consider well 1 Cor. 14:15–19; wherefore let them keep silence in the church, and in its parts, when assembled to worship God. In all public worship by prayer, teaching is set on foot two ways: first, by proposing to the assembly the things that must, by agreement, be prayed for; second, by proving them to suit the will of God, that prayer may be made in faith (1 John 5:14). For all such prayer must be for things first agreed upon, and consequently for things proved good and suitable by the Word — for those who meet for prayer without this pray at random, not by rule. "If two of you shall agree on earth touching anything they shall ask, according to God's will, it shall be done for them of my Father in heaven," says Christ (Matt. 18:19). If things prayed for in assemblies must first be jointly agreed upon, then they must first be proposed, expounded, and proved good by someone in that assembly — and if this is not teaching, I am mistaken. This must be done to instruct all present, help their faith, and quicken their spirits, that they may, as one man, have their eyes toward the Lord (Zech. 9:1). But that this power is given to women, even the most highly regarded ordinary believers, I do not believe. I do not believe they should minister to God in prayer before the whole church — for then I should be a Ranter or a Quaker — nor do I believe they should do it in their own women's assembly, for the reason urged before.

Thirdly, the Holy Ghost particularly insists on the inability of women to manage well the worship now under consideration, and therefore it ought not to be presumed upon by them. They are forbidden to teach, even to speak, in the church of God — and why forbidden, but because of this inability? They cannot orderly manage the worship that is to be performed before God in assemblies (I speak of our ordinary believing women; I know of none extraordinary among the churches). They are not built to manage such worship; they are not the image and glory of God as the men are (1 Cor. 11:7), but are placed beneath, and called the glory of the man. When our first mother, who was not burdened with the weaknesses our women now carry, stepped out of her place to speak a word for worship, she was utterly baffled and failed in the attempt — and so thoroughly that she overthrew, at one stroke, not only the reputation of women, but her own soul, her husband, and the whole world besides (Gen. 3:1–7). The fallen angel knew what he was doing when he assaulted the woman: his cunning told him she was the weaker vessel, and that the man was made head in worship and keeper of God's garden (Gen. 2:15–17) — so he came to the woman instead, and so overcame the world. It is from this consideration that Paul tells Timothy he does not permit a woman to teach, or to usurp authority over the man, but to be in silence, giving the reason: Adam was formed first, then Eve — and so had the headship in worship — and Adam was not deceived, but the woman, being deceived, was in the transgression (1 Tim. 2:13–14).

Fourthly, if the weight of this worship is, as indeed it is, so great that the strongest and most able, engaging with good and lawful authority, usually come off blushing and repenting of their shortcomings — what will happen to those much weaker, when, like Eve, they set to it by way of usurping authority and their own will? You know what it cost Nadab and Abihu, sons of Aaron though they were, to offer strange fire (a type of prayer). Mr. K. praises the sisters, the women's meetings, and how they have prevailed with heaven — poor man, I am sorry for his weakness, that he should show himself so soft in such a day as this. As all worship in assemblies ought to be performed with the most exact order and solemnity, prayer especially so, perhaps more than any other part, which makes it a heavier matter still. When men preach, they deal with men; but when they pray in assemblies, they deal with both men and God at once — speaking to God by petition, and to men by instruction. I am persuaded that if those most fond of women's prayer meetings were petitioning the king for their lives, they would not set women to be their advocates before him — especially if the king had declared by law that he would not permit a woman to speak before him in open court. There are also many temptations attending the duty of praying in assemblies, especially for those immediately engaged in it, which they must wrestle with and groan under. We do not put the weak upon this service — not even weak men, in the presence of the strong. How then should the weakest of all be put upon it, and that when gathered by themselves? Men, though strong and acting by lawful authority, can scarcely do it without unutterable groans — how then shall those who attempt it without that authority perform it as acceptable worship to God? This work, then, is as much too heavy for our women now as the work Eve engaged in at first was too heavy for her.

Fifthly, if this worship may be managed by the sisterhood of the churches, gathered together in the absence of their men, of what significance is it that man is made the head of the woman, as much in worship as in nature (1 Cor. 11:3, 7)? Moreover, why are the elders of the churches called watchmen, overseers, guides, teachers, and rulers, if this kind of worship may be performed without their conduct and government (Ezek. 3:7, 33:7, Acts 20:28, Eph. 4:11, Psalm 78:72, Heb. 13:17)? Why is man made the head of the woman in the very worship now under debate, and why are women commanded silence there, if they may congregate by themselves and set up worship of their own? Worship was ordained before woman was made, so the word of God at first came not immediately to her, but to him who was first formed and made head in worship (Gen. 2:16–18, 1 Cor. 14:35–36). This is why women are so strictly tied to this headship that, if they wish to learn, they must ask their husbands at home, not appoint meetings of their own sex to teach one another. But what of those with unbelieving husbands, or none at all? Let them attend the ordinances God has appointed for building up the body of Christ, and learn as the angels do (Eph. 4:11–13, 3:10, 1 Pet. 1:12). But I say, if they must, as Mr. K. claims they are duty-bound to, meet apart from their men and there perform this most solemn worship: how shall the elders and overseers perform their duty in this, since they are entirely shut out of it? Unless it be said that watching and guiding the manner of worship in assemblies is no part of the overseer's work. No faithful overseer would leave worship, even in the least part of the congregation, to be performed by any weak brother without warrant — though I believe it might more reasonably be left to weak brethren than to the strongest among our ordinary women. Our elders, wanting our worship performed by the most able when unbelievers are watching, would hardly be so unwise as to turn loose a company of weak women to be preyed upon by fallen angels. In conclusion, I will ask this man: does he not, by pleading for these women's meetings, declare that women without their men are better able to maintain divine worship than men are without their elders? For he himself will not allow that men should always perform worship without his oversight, and yet he pleads that women should have such worship in their congregation among themselves, excluding the men from it forever — for, he says, the Spirit is given them to that very end, that they may meet together to pray apart, without their men.

%% section: scripture-objections
%% number: 
%% title: Answering the Six Scripture Proofs

Objection 1: Miriam took a timbrel and went out, and all the women followed her, praising God with timbrels and dances for their deliverance. Therefore the women of the churches of Christ may appoint meetings of their own, separate from their brethren, and there perform divine worship without their men (Exod. 15:20–21). Answer: Miriam was a prophetess, and I suppose none of our women will pretend to be such. Though Mr. K. labors to get around this by saying the work of praising was incumbent on all, judgment, discretion, and a spirit of conduct suitable to the duty was found, among the women, in none but her — why else is it said that Miriam led them forth? Any other woman, by Mr. K.'s argument, might have done it as well; thus he degrades the prophetess to win favor with ordinary women. But his point carries no weight: the women were not left, in that extraordinary service, to the spirit of ordinary believers, and it is crooked dealing to use extraordinary persons in extraordinary acts to prove it lawful for ordinary persons to do what is not commanded them. And though Miriam went out with the women, it was not away from the men into some remote place to worship by themselves — rather she and the women followed after Moses, singing as befit her sex, for even as an extraordinary woman she could not make herself equal to Moses and Aaron, and so came behind in worship, yet with the whole body of the people (v. 22). Women, even prophetesses, must wear some badge of inferiority to those who are prophets indeed (1 Cor. 11:3–8). This Scripture, therefore, does not favor this man's opinion, that it is the duty of women of the churches of Christ to separate from their brethren and perform divine worship by themselves.

Objection 2: Esther the queen performed this duty of prayer with her maidens, without their men; therefore the women of Gospel churches may separate from their brethren and perform it among themselves (Est. 4:16). Answer: Esther was in the king's palace and could not at that time come to her brethren — not even to her uncle Mordecai, with whom she had to communicate through Hatach, the king's attendant. She could by no means at that time have communion with the church; no wonder, then, if she fasted with her maidens alone, for that was all she could do. But I ask our arguer: did Esther count it a burden or a privilege to be thus separated from her brethren and forced to this work? If a privilege, let him prove it; if a burden, he has little cause to make her practice a ground for women who are at liberty to separate from their brethren for such worship. We do not read that she asked any woman at liberty to leave the men to join her, from which we may gather that she preferred their liberty to worship with men far above a women's meeting; she counted it bondage enough that she and her maidens were confined to it. Nor did she attempt to take that unavoidable work upon herself without asking the men's help, begging that she might be borne up by their faith and prayers (vv. 15–16) — plainly concluding that she counted such work too hard for women to perform without the help of their men. Besides, this "women's meeting" consisted of none but the queen and a few of her household maids. What if Esther did pray with her maids in her chamber, because she could not come out to her brethren — is it fair to make the necessity of a woman in confinement a law for women at liberty? This argument is therefore mistaken, and this text cannot support it.

Objection 3: the prophet Zechariah says the Spirit is promised, in New Testament times, to women, that they may pray together apart from men (Zech. 12:11–13). Answer: the text says no such thing, but is greatly abused by this man. It does say their wives shall mourn apart, but it does not say they shall do so together, nor that they shall separate themselves by God's command from their brethren to do so — the text knows nothing of that. My reason against him is this: the "look" upon him whom we have pierced, which is to cause this mourning, comes by an immediate revelation of the Holy Ghost, who does not announce beforehand when he will come upon us. But a meeting such as Mr. K. intends must be the product of arrangement and time. God says he will pour out the Spirit of grace and supplication on the house of David, and then they shall look — that is, when the Spirit so works in them as to enable it. Since this mourning is the effect of that look, and comes unawares, can Mr. K. prove that these women were to have advance notice of when they should have this look? His conclusion proves only that he is ignorant of the work of the Spirit in this matter, or that his fondness for women's meetings has made him forget his own experience. That all these women should have this look at the same time, so that they might, by the direction of the Holy Ghost, separate themselves from their men to hold a women's meeting for this purpose, is more fictitious than one would imagine a man should dream up. Rather, I take this mourning to be as another prophet says: they shall be like doves of the valleys upon the mountains, all of them mourning, everyone for his own iniquity (Ezek. 7:16). All those counted worthy of this look shall mourn apart, or by themselves, when they have it — and this is the mourning that shall be in the house of David, Jerusalem, the church, both men and women, whenever the Holy Ghost helps us look upon him whom we have pierced.

Objection 4: women used to meet together to pray in Gospel times; therefore women in Gospel churches may separate from their brethren to perform divine worship by themselves, without their men (Acts 16:13). Answer: it is not said that the women of churches met together alone to pray, but that Paul went down to a riverside where prayer was customarily made, and spoke to the women who resorted there. It seems most agreeable to the Word that the Law was read there by Jewish priests to the proselyte women of that city, with prayer, as was customary, intermixed — though this is only conjecture. At that time there was no Gospel church of Christ there, nor any Gospel ministry before it, and consequently no church obedience. Even granting that only women were at that meeting, and that their custom was to meet at that riverside to pray, it does not follow that their practice should be a pattern or law for women in churches to separate from their brethren to worship by themselves. There was no Gospel believer there — Lydia herself, before Paul came, had her heart shut against the faith of Christ — and I do not understand how a company of strangers to the Gospel faith should be a pattern of Christian worship for women in churches. If Paul's call to Philippi had come through the vision of a woman, or a women's meeting, what an argument this man would have drawn from it! But since it was by a man, he has lost that argument. Paul went to that company to preach Christ's Gospel to them, not because they merited his coming, but of God's grace, just as Peter and John went up to the temple at the hour of prayer, and Paul to the synagogue at Antioch. That this women's meeting should be so deserving, while its members were without faith in Christ, as to procure a Gospel minister sent to them, and yet that so few of them should be converted, seems to me a greater paradox — for we do not read that any woman there, save Lydia, was ever converted to Christ. Lydia worshipped God, but she was only a proselyte, and knew no more of Christ than the eunuch did. Even granting she had faith, does that make every practice acceptable, or a law and commandment to others whose work is not meritorious at all?

Objection 5: Mr. K. says Malachi 3:16 supports these meetings. Answer: not at all — though Mr. K. has taken the liberty of changing a term in the text to make it say what he wants, putting out "thought" and putting in "call." But it will not do his work, for however he alters it, that Scripture will not say it is the duty of women in Gospel churches to separate from their brethren to perform divine worship among themselves.

Objection 6: Jude 20 justifies these meetings, unless, he says, someone will claim women are not to be built up in their most holy faith. Answer: how eagerly this man would lay hold of something, if only he had the Word of God to found it upon. Does the apostle there treat at all of women and their meetings, or is he addressing the Beloved generally, to be built up? Does he not there speak to the church, which consists of men and women together? Can women be built up in their most holy faith no other way but by meetings of their own without their men? "Building up yourselves," I suppose, is the phrase he leans on — but cannot the church, and every woman in it, build up herself without a women's meeting? Do they not have the Word, their closet, and the grace of meditation, for that very purpose? He does not even say "build up one another," but if he had, it might well have been done without a women's meeting. This text, then, is written to the church of Christ, exhorting it to faith and prayer, but it says not a word of a women's meeting, and it is trifling with the Word to suggest otherwise.

%% section: picking-bones
%% number: 
%% title: Picking the Bones of the Arguments

Having taken from his arguments those holy words of God which he has abused to make them stand, I come next to the arguments themselves, and intend to pick their bones for the crows. First, he says the same Spirit that was in Miriam is also in all God's servants for the same end, both to pray for mercies we need and to praise God for mercies received. Answer: the question is whether Miriam led out the women only as an ordinary saint. If you evade this, you choose the tongue of the crafty, for she managed that work as Miriam the prophetess — as Miriam the prophetess, she did not lead the women away from their men to worship remotely by themselves, as shown before. Second, he says God has promised to pour out his Spirit in Gospel times to the very end that women might pray together apart from men. Answer: if by "men" he means the brethren, the prophet will not vouch for him, for he neither says nor implies any such thing — and this comes uncomfortably close to making God and his prophet the author of schism in worship. Third, he says God so approved of women meeting together to pray in Gospel times as to use the occasion to make known his mind concerning Jesus Christ (Acts 16:13). Answer: if this were true, the Popish doctrine of merit — works done before faith — would be good, for we read that none of those women save Lydia even feared or worshipped God. Fourth, he says Esther and her maids fasted and prayed, and the Lord gave a gracious answer to the church, then under Haman's rage. Answer: did not Mordecai and the good men also pray and fast? Might they not obtain some part of the mercy as well as those women? And is it not deceitful arguing, tending to puff up that sex, to credit the deliverance to the queen's and her maids' prayers alone, when the text says no such thing? Fifth, he appeals to the women, asking whether they have not, by their prayers in these meetings, obtained many blessed returns from God. Answer: I count this no better than the worst part of his paper, for besides the silliness of making these good women judges in their own cause, his words tend directly to puff them up to their destruction. I have sometimes wondered that when something extraordinary happens for the good of God's church, a few women meeting to pray should fancy that they alone fetched the benefit down from heaven, when perhaps ten thousand men in the land prayed for the same mercy as hard as they. Poor women — you are to be pitied; your tempter, this man and his fellows, is to bear the blame.

%% section: further-objections
%% number: 
%% title: Three More Objections Answered

Objection 1: "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, I am there in the midst of them" (Matt. 18:20). Answer: to gather in Christ's name is to gather by his authority — that is, by his law and commandment (Acts 4:17–18, 30, 5:28, 40, Col. 3:17). But we have no law or commandment of Christ that the women of this or that church should separate themselves from their brethren to hold meetings of their own for divine worship, and therefore such meetings cannot be in his name, by his authority, law, and commandment — and so ought not to be at all. Objection 2: but women may, if sent for by others of their own sex when sick, come together and pray in that gathering before they part. Answer: the law of Christ is, "Is any sick among you? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him" — and to this command a threefold promise is attached: the prayer of faith shall save the sick, the Lord shall raise him up, and if he has committed sin it shall be forgiven him (James 5:14–15). Given that this advice comes with such grace attached, I think it best in all such cases to make the Word of God our rule. Objection 3: but women sometimes have matters which modesty will not allow to be made known to men — what must they do then? Answer: a wife and her husband are one flesh, no longer to be counted two; let her tell her grief to him. So Rachel asked children of her husband, and did not go to a gathering of women to complain to them (Gen. 30:1); or let her betake herself to her closet, as Rebecca did (Gen. 25:20–23); or, if in the assembly of the saints, let her pray in her heart, as Hannah did — and if her petition is lawful, I doubt not she may be heard (1 Sam. 1:13).

%% section: cautionary-conclusion
%% number: 
%% title: A Cautionary Conclusion

Take heed of letting the name or good appearance of a thing beget in your heart a religious reverence for it; look instead to the Word for your foundation, for it is the Word that authorizes whatever may be done with warrant in worship — without the Word, things are of human invention, however splendid or beautiful they may appear. Without doubt the friars and nuns, and their religious orders, were of good intent at first, as also the compulsory vows of chastity, single life, and the like; but they were all without the Word, and so, as their foundation lacked divine authority, their practice lacked sanctity from the Holy Ghost. The word "prayer" itself seems so holy in appearance that one who questions it seems almost devilish — and yet we find that prayers have been misused and disorderly, and so may, without danger of damnation, be called into question, and set back in order (Matt. 6:5–8, 23:14, James 4:3). I am not among those who say, "What profit is it that we should pray to God?" (Job 21:15). But finding no good footing in the Word for the kind of service treated above, and knowing that error and human invention in religion never present themselves except with a wiped mouth and as demure a face as possible, and being persuaded that this opinion of Mr. K. is unfounded, a stranger to the Scriptures, I, as an officer of the church, have apprehended it and put it in the stocks, and will keep it there until I see by what authority it has leave to pass and repass as it pleases among the godly in this land.

Yet by all I have said, I never meant to suggest in the least that believing women are not saints as well as men, and members of the body of Christ. I will add that, as they and we are united to Christ and made members of his mystical body, there is no superiority, as far as I know, but we are all one in Christ — for the man is not without the woman, nor the woman without the man, in the Lord, and we are not counted as male or female in him (1 Cor. 11:11, Gal. 3:28, Eph. 1:23). Only note that this is spoken of that church which is his true mystical body, not of every particular congregation of professing Christians. As Christ has a mystical body, called his members, his flesh and bones (Eph. 5:30), so he also has a body politic — congregations formed by the skill his ministers have in his Word, for upholding his name and preserving his glory in the world against Antichrist. In this church, order and discipline for the nourishing of the true mystical body of Christ have been established since the foundation of the world. And where there is order and government by laws, there must necessarily also be a distinction of sex, degree, and age, and offices and officers for our furtherance and joy of faith — from which government our ordinary women are excluded by Paul, nor should this, being the wisdom of God, give them any offense. Wherefore, my beloved sisters, this inferiority of yours will last but a little while: when the day of God's salvation comes, these distinctions of sex shall be laid aside, and every vessel shall be filled to the brim — for you shall be saved and gathered up to that state of happiness, if you continue in faith, charity, and holiness, with sobriety (1 Tim. 2:15).

%% section: to-good-women
%% number: 
%% title: To the Good Women in the Churches

First, these meetings of yours, honorable women, in which you attempt to perform divine worship by yourselves without your men, lacking the authority of the Word to sanctify them, will be found will-worship on the day you are measured by the golden reed of God's law (Isa. 1:12, Rev. 11:1–3). Second, these meetings of yours are unnecessary; there are elders or brethren in all churches to call and manage this worship of God, if you abide in your subjection and worship as you are commanded. Third, these meetings of yours, instead of being an ornament to the church you are in, are a shame and blemish to it, for they show either the unruliness of such women or that the church lacks the skill to govern them — have you no man among your number? (1 Cor. 11:4–5, Mal. 1:14). Fourth, even supposing your meetings were in some cases lawful, since they may be managed better by the brethren, you and your meetings ought to give place. That the church together, and the brethren as the mouth to God, are best able to manage this solemn worship, consider: the gifts for such service are found most in the elders and leading men of the church, not in the women; the spirit for conduct and government in that worship is not in the women but the men; the men are permitted in such worship to stand with open face before God, a thing denied the women (1 Cor. 11:4–5); and wherever meetings for prayer are commanded in Scripture, men are named as the mouth to God, never an ordinary woman (Joel 1:13–14, 2:15–17); and the encouragement to pray believingly in assemblies is given to men — men, therefore, in all assemblies for worship, should be the ones to manage it, and let others join in their place.

Fifthly, women are an ornament in the church of God on earth, as the angels are in the church in heaven — between whom there is also some comparison, for they cover their faces in acts of worship (Isa. 6:2, 1 Cor. 11:10). But as the angels in heaven are not Christ, and so are not admitted to the mercy seat to speak to God, neither are women on earth the man, who is to worship with open face before him and be the mouth in prayer for the rest. As the angels cry "Holy, holy, holy" with faces covered in heaven, so let the women cry "Holy, holy, holy" with faces covered on earth. Methinks, holy and beloved sisters, you should be content to wear this token of your position, since its cause arose at first from yourselves — it was the woman the serpent first made use of, by whom he overthrew the world; wherefore the woman, to the world's end, must wear tokens of her position in all matters of worship: her silence, and a covering for her face, in token of it, whenever the church comes together to worship (Gen. 3:16, 1 Tim. 2:15, 1 Cor. 11:13, 1 Tim. 2:9). Modesty and shame-facedness become women at all times, especially in public worship, and the more of this is mixed with their grace and bearing, the more beautiful they are, both to God and men. Sixthly, in what I have said about women's meetings, I have not at all concerned myself with those extraordinary women such as Miriam, Deborah, Huldah, Anna, the daughters of Philip the Evangelist, Priscilla, or the like, who might teach, prophesy, and had power to call the people together to do so. Though I must say of them, they still bore, notwithstanding so high a calling, the badge of their inferiority to those who were prophets indeed — hence it is said that if they prayed or prophesied in the church with their heads uncovered, they dishonored their head (1 Cor. 11:5). Yet I will say, if any of these extraordinary women had separated themselves and other women apart to worship by themselves, and had commanded it to be done in God's name, I would freely have consented that our women should do so too, when led by so extraordinary a one. But finding nothing like this in the Word of God to sanctify such a practice, and all this being urged by a man of mere conceit, I found myself obliged to say something to suppress his opinion.

But to return to the good women in the churches, and to conclude my discourse with them: be as often in your closets as you will — the oftener there, the better. This is your duty, this is your privilege; this place is sanctified to you for service by the Holy Word of God. Here you may pray without making ordinances interfere, without presuming upon the power of your superiors, and without pushing your brethren behind you in worship. Be also as often as possible in worship, when the church, or parts of it, are assembled for that purpose, and when there, join with heart and soul with your brethren in all holy petitions to God. Let the men be the mouth to God in prayer, and the women follow after with groans and desires; let the men stand with open face in this worship, being the image and glory of God, and let the women be clothed in modest apparel, with shame-facedness, in remembrance of what has been touched on before. When women keep their places, and men manage the worship of God as they should, we shall have better days for the church of God in the world (Jer. 29:10–13). Women are not to be blamed for being eager to pray to God — only let them know their bounds; and I wish that idleness in men is not the real cause of putting this work upon their good women. Surely those who can scarcely tie their shoes before heading to the tavern or the coffee-house door in the morning can scarcely spare time to be a while in their closets with God. Morning closet-prayers are now, by most London professors, thrown away, and what kind they make at night, God now knows; however, I have cause, as to this, to look at my own house first. I have said what I meant to say, though there are some other matters concerning women on which, when I have opportunity, I may also give my judgment. For the present, I entreat that these lines be taken in good part, for I seek edification, not contention. FINIS.
