%% section: epistle
%% number: 
%% title: The Epistle Dedicatory

I don't say any of this to put you down. I know you're loved by God because of Christ, and that your faith rests on the very same foundation ours does. God doesn't play favorites between men and women when it comes to his saving grace — plenty of ordinary Christian women have outdone their brothers in holiness of life. That only reflects well on you, and should convict those of us men who've fallen short by comparison. In spiritual devotion, as in natural affection, women often outdo men.

When Christ walked the earth, no man did more for him than the sinful woman who anointed him, or Joanna, Susanna, and others like them (Luke 7:36–38, 8:1–3). Women have shown the same courage as men, too, when called to stand up for Christ's name publicly — history bears that out. Among those tortured for their faith who refused an easy way out, holding out for a better resurrection, there were women as well as men (Hebrews 11:35). So I genuinely honor your virtue, and I hope your example spurs me on to greater holiness too.

You'll see in what follows that I do have real reasons for questioning whether it's right for you to hold worship meetings on your own, without the men. But I don't think this is really your fault. I blame your leaders for it — whether they overestimate your abilities, think too highly of you compared to themselves, or are just carried away with misplaced enthusiasm, I won't try to guess. But I'm convinced they've talked you into taking on more than you should.

I know full well that writing this — even though I mean it for your good and for good order — is going to get me criticized, maybe harshly, by some of you, and I expect Mr. K especially will take it personally, since I'm accusing him of mishandling Scripture. I'll probably be called against prayer and against prayer meetings altogether, which isn't true. I'll try to take the criticism patiently and to genuinely care about the people who end up upset with me over it.

I wouldn't have said anything at all about this if Mr. K. hadn't circulated his paper among us, promoting this practice in a way that threatened our peace. I don't think he meant to cause a split, but his arguments pointed that way — and if our congregation weren't naturally sensible and even-tempered, it could easily have set off real conflict. Thankfully we're at peace and still walking in love, despite the pressure to do otherwise. Some people have also started talking against me over this, looking for any excuse — to them I'm showing, in what follows, that I had good reason for what I did.

I commit you to God and to his word of grace, which can build you up and give you a place among those made holy by faith in Jesus Christ — to whom be honor and glory forever. I remain your faithful friend and brother: praying for you, loving you in the gospel, and ready to serve you however I can. John Bunyan.

%% section: occasion
%% number: 
%% title: How This Controversy Began

Here's how I got drawn into this: I'd looked into the so-called women's prayer meetings and concluded there was no scriptural basis for them, so I raised the question within our own fellowship. When I was asked for my reasons, I gave them, and the practice stopped among us — our women were genuinely willing to give up something they couldn't find biblical grounds for. But somehow word of this reached Mr. K., who — though I don't think he understood the actual debate — wrote up four arguments defending the practice. He didn't send them to me directly; they circulated among people who shared his view, and it was about two years before I finally received them, along with this note:

Brother Bunyan — we're sending you this on behalf of some godly women who've long made a habit of meeting to pray. They heard you disagree with the practice, and wanted you to see this paper. It's from Mr. K., who'd like to know your objections and is ready to discuss it further. Please write out your answer — Mr. K. is expecting it — and leave it with S. F. in Bedford. Your friends in the Lord, S. B. and S. F.

Once I had the papers in hand, I wasn't sure at first whether this was genuine or made up, so I wrote back to the women to check. But then I remembered I'd heard something like this before, so I decided it was real and that I should answer it properly. So, without further preamble, here are the arguments he sent, followed by how I responded to them.

%% section: mrk-arguments
%% number: 
%% title: Mr. K.'s Arguments for Women's Prayer Meetings

He opens by asking whether God-fearing women may meet to pray together, and whether that's lawful. Then he expresses amazement that anyone who fears God would even question it — after all, the command to pray applies to everyone, and Scripture gives plenty of examples to back the practice up.

His first argument: if women can praise God together for mercies received, they can pray together too — Exodus 15:20–21 proves it, where Miriam and the women praised God after the crossing of the Red Sea. He anticipates the objection that this was an extraordinary case, with Miriam an extraordinary person (a prophetess), and answers that our own dangers today are just as extraordinary as Pharaoh's threat was then, so if God delivers us, that too will call for praise. As for Miriam's special status, he says praising God for mercy received is everyone's duty, since everyone shares in the mercy — and the same Spirit that was in Miriam is in all God's people, for praying as much as for praising.

Second argument: Esther and her maids fasted and prayed together when the Jews were in mortal danger, and God answered and delivered them (Esther 4:16). Since God approved of that, women praying together must be acceptable.

Third argument: God promised, in Zechariah 12:10–13, to pour out his Spirit in the gospel era specifically so that women could pray apart from men — the text even uses the plural, 'they shall mourn apart, their wives apart,' not just 'he apart, his wife apart.' And Malachi 3:16 says God notices those who speak often together and call on his name. So this isn't just something women may do, but something they should do.

Fourth argument: In Acts 16:13, women meeting to pray became the very occasion God used to reveal Christ to Lydia. God clearly approved of that gathering, so it must be lawful.

That's the end of his four arguments. He closes by adding: besides all these specific examples, doesn't Jude 20's call to build one another up in faith and pray in the Holy Spirit apply to women just as much as men? So don't let anyone stop you from a duty that's especially yours in a time like this. Haven't many women found real blessing through this practice, both for themselves and the church? What God has clearly blessed, no man should discourage. Go check the verses yourself — I think they'll convince you.

%% section: answer-opening
%% number: 
%% title: Bunyan's Opening Remarks

That's his case. And frankly, I see both arrogance and flattery in it — arrogance, in blaming Scripture itself for his own misreading of it; flattery, in telling people exactly what they want to hear and letting them be the judges of their own case. I'll show why as I go.

Since his supporters told me he expects an answer, I feel obligated to point out where he's wrong. He wants me confined to a written reply, but since his own paper has been circulating who-knows-where, the only way I can counter it is by circulating mine just as widely. And I won't be bound to his style of argument either — I'll admit up front I don't know the formal logic of syllogisms, majors and minors and all that. I just don't see much sense, and even less truth, in his case. He's twisted Scripture to prop up his pet theory. So I'll first separate these Bible texts from the errors he's attached to them, then go through his arguments point by point.

And rather than answer his question exactly as he framed it, I'll pose the real question we actually debated in our own fellowship: is it the duty of the women in a congregation to regularly and formally separate from the men to hold their own worship — prayer and the like — without them? That's what we actually argued about, and Mr. K. could have asked us about it directly if he really wanted to engage.

Reader, here's how I worked through this with my own congregation: first, I defined the terms of the question. Second, I looked at what kinds of gatherings were actually used for worship. Third, I looked at whose prayers were actually used, or called for, in those gatherings.

%% section: stating-question
%% number: 
%% title: Stating the Question

First, my definitions: by "a church of Christ" I mean a congregation actually organized around and following Scripture. By "situated" I just mean wherever that church happens to be, whole or in part. By "separating" I mean women arranging their own meetings, apart from the men, to conduct worship — prayer specifically — on their own.

With those terms settled, let's look at what kinds of gatherings were actually used for worship in Scripture. I find three sorts: first, mixed gatherings of believers and unbelievers together, where believers were built up and unbelievers convicted and converted — this pattern shows up repeatedly across the Gospels and in 1 Corinthians 14:24. Second, the church meeting on its own, apart from outsiders (Acts 12, 13, 15, 20; John 20). Third, gatherings of just the elders and leading men of the church, with the rest of the congregation absent (Matthew 10, Luke 9, Acts 1–2, Galatians 2).

But nowhere in Scripture do I find women of the church habitually separating from the men to hold their own worship, nor any biblical warrant for arranging to do so. So as far as I can tell, this is a human invention — and Mr. K. is its promoter.

Third, whose prayers were actually voiced in those lawful assemblies? Whenever Scripture mentions it, it's either the church as a whole or its leading men (Judges 2, 20; Joel 1–2; Acts 12–13). And whenever Paul or others asked people to pray for them, they asked the church generally or the brethren specifically — never a women's meeting. When Paul names people to pray, it's always men (1 Thessalonians 5, 2 Thessalonians 3, Romans 15, 1 Timothy 2). This pattern isn't unique to Paul either: Christ's parable says men ought always to pray; James speaks of a righteous man's prayer; Moses sent young men to sacrifice; the people in Zechariah's day sent their men to pray. None of this looks down on women's prayers — it just shows who Scripture consistently puts forward as the one who prays aloud in a gathered assembly.

One more point: in Jeremiah's day, when women were confronted for baking cakes to the "queen of heaven," their defense wasn't that the act itself was right — it was that they hadn't done it without their husbands' knowledge (Jeremiah 44:17–19). Even then, acting apart from the men wasn't treated as a legitimate defense on its own.

So, to the actual question: is it a woman's duty to regularly and formally separate from the men to hold her own worship? My answer is no. There's no command for it in the New Testament, and no example of it either. Without either, it isn't a duty — it isn't even a liberty. Attempting it is presumption.

Mr. K. says the command to pray is general — it applies to everyone. True, but the way it's carried out isn't unlimited. Women should pray, no question. But does that mean they should be the ones leading public prayer aloud before everyone? No — and yet the command to pray is still general to all. Likewise, women aren't commanded to separate from the men to pray in their own company, even though the general command to pray still applies to them. Who prays, and how, are different questions from whether someone should pray at all — and Mr. K. knows as well as I do that the manner and order of worship matters, not just the fact of it.

%% section: five-reasons
%% number: 
%% title: Five Reasons for Dissenting

First reason: calling a meeting for worship — whether the whole church or part of it — is an exercise of authority, and that authority rests with the elders or with the church as a whole, never with women acting on their own. Mr. K. actually implies women do have this authority, since he says God's Spirit is given to them specifically so they can pray apart from men — but if that's true, they'd have the standing to summon their own sex to meet, and it's obvious where that leads. Throughout Scripture, calling the church together for worship was always done by recognized leaders — Moses, Aaron, and the priests in the Old Testament, then elders and leading men later (Joshua 24, Ezra 10, Acts 14–15). Even where it wasn't the elders alone, it was still the church's collective authority at work (Judges 20, Ezra 3, Acts 12, 1 Corinthians 5 and 11). I never find ordinary women claiming that authority for themselves. The gathering in Acts 12:12 was arranged by the church's own authority, meeting in different homes out of necessity during persecution — not by any individual's say-so. I have no problem with part of a congregation meeting for prayer without the elders present, as long as the men leading it are approved by the church leadership. But sisters calling their own sex together to run worship on their own — I find no scriptural basis for that at all.

Second reason: praying aloud in front of a group isn't just talking to God — it's teaching the people listening. Whoever leads a public prayer is instructing the assembly as much as petitioning heaven, and 1 Corinthians 14:15–19 makes clear women are to keep silent in that setting. Think about how public prayer actually works: someone has to propose what should be prayed for, and show from Scripture that it's the right thing to ask for, so the prayer is made in faith. That's teaching, plain and simple — it instructs everyone present and builds their faith. I don't believe ordinary women, however well-regarded, have been given that role. If I said they should lead prayer before the whole church, I might as well be a Ranter or a Quaker. And I don't believe it's any more appropriate in an all-women meeting, for the same reason.

Third reason: Scripture is direct about women's unsuitedness to lead this kind of worship, which is exactly why it shouldn't be assumed. Women are told not to teach or speak in church — and the reason given is precisely this unsuitedness. When Eve, who didn't carry the weaknesses our women carry now, stepped outside her role to speak up about worship, she failed completely — and that single failure brought down not just her own standing but her husband and the whole world with her (Genesis 3). Satan knew exactly what he was doing: he understood the man was meant to lead, so he targeted the woman instead. That's exactly why Paul tells Timothy that women shouldn't teach or have authority over men, but should remain quiet — and his reasoning is that Adam was formed first, and Adam wasn't deceived, but the woman was (1 Timothy 2:13–14).

Fourth reason: leading worship is such a weighty responsibility that even the strongest, most qualified men, doing it with proper authority, usually come away humbled by how short they fell. So what happens when much weaker people attempt it by usurping authority that isn't theirs, the way Eve did? Remember what it cost Nadab and Abihu — Aaron's own sons — to offer unauthorized fire in worship. Mr. K. keeps praising the women's meetings and how effective they've been; frankly, that kind of talk is soft-headed for a serious man in serious times. Prayer in a public assembly is arguably the weightiest part of worship, because the one praying is dealing with both God and the listening congregation at once — petitioning God and instructing the people simultaneously. If the people most enthusiastic about women's prayer meetings had to petition a king for their lives, they wouldn't send women to plead their case — especially if the king had explicitly ruled women out of speaking in his court. And there are real spiritual pressures that come with praying aloud before others — pressures we don't put on the weak, even weak men, let alone the weakest among us, meeting entirely on their own. If strong men, acting with proper authority, can barely manage it without deep inward struggle, how can anyone expect it to go well when done without that authority at all? This is as far beyond our women now as leading worship was beyond Eve then.

Fifth reason: if the sisterhood can run this worship entirely without the men present, what's left of the idea that man is the head of woman, in worship as in nature (1 Corinthians 11)? And why do we even have elders — called watchmen, overseers, guides, rulers — if this kind of worship can happen with no oversight from them at all? Worship was instituted before woman was created, and headship in worship belongs to the one formed first. That's exactly why Paul tells women to ask their husbands at home rather than set up teaching meetings of their own (1 Corinthians 14:35). And for those with unbelieving husbands, or none — they have the regular ministry of the church to grow under; they don't need a workaround. If Mr. K. is right that women must meet apart from men for this kind of worship, then the elders are completely shut out of overseeing it — unless we say overseeing worship isn't actually part of an elder's job. No responsible overseer would leave any part of worship to be run by an unqualified man, let alone hand it to a group of women with no oversight at all, especially when we're supposedly concerned about how it looks to unbelievers watching. Really, by defending these meetings, Mr. K. is effectively claiming that women without men can run worship better than men can without their elders — since he himself wouldn't let men worship entirely without his oversight, yet he wants women to have worship among themselves with the men permanently excluded.

%% section: scripture-objections
%% number: 
%% title: Answering the Six Scripture Proofs

Objection 1 — Miriam took a timbrel, and all the women followed her, celebrating their deliverance with music and dancing (Exodus 15:20–21). Doesn't that justify women's meetings? My answer: Miriam was a prophetess, and I doubt any of our women would claim that title. Mr. K. tries to get around this by saying praising God was everyone's job — but Scripture credits the leadership specifically to Miriam, not to women generally, which suggests it took her particular gifting, not just any woman's initiative. It's not fair reasoning to take an extraordinary person's extraordinary act and use it to justify ordinary people doing something they were never commanded to do. And besides, Miriam didn't lead the women off somewhere private away from the men — they followed Moses out from the sea, singing along with everyone, with Miriam herself still deferring to Moses and Aaron. Even an exceptional woman like Miriam wore some mark of being under male leadership (1 Corinthians 11:3–8). So this passage doesn't actually support women's meetings apart from men.

Objection 2 — Esther and her maids fasted and prayed without the men, so women's meetings must be fine (Esther 4:16). Answer: Esther was confined in the palace and literally couldn't reach the men — she had to communicate with her own uncle through a go-between. She wasn't choosing separation; it was forced on her by circumstance. Ask yourself: did Esther treat this as a privilege or a burden? Nothing suggests she saw it as ideal — in fact she still asked Mordecai and the Jewish men to fast and support her in prayer too (Esther 4:15–16), which shows she didn't think women praying alone was sufficient on its own. And this "meeting" was just the queen and a couple of maids, forced together by her particular circumstances — not a template for women generally. Using an emergency situation someone was stuck in to justify something people are choosing to do freely isn't a fair argument.

Objection 3 — Zechariah 12:11–13 promises the Spirit to women so they can pray apart from men. Answer: the text doesn't say that at all; Mr. K. is stretching it. It says wives will mourn "apart" — meaning each mourns individually over their own sin, in private — not that they're commanded to organize group meetings. This kind of mourning comes as a spontaneous work of the Holy Spirit convicting someone of sin, not something you can schedule in advance the way an organized meeting requires. The idea that every woman in Jerusalem would have this exact spiritual experience at the same moment, prompting them all to break off and hold a women's meeting together, is simply not what the text describes — it's closer to individual, private repentance (compare Ezekiel 7:16, where everyone mourns "every one for his own iniquity"). This kind of mourning happens to men and women alike, individually, whenever the Spirit moves someone to genuine repentance — it isn't a blueprint for a standing women's prayer group.

Objection 4 — Acts 16:13 shows women meeting to pray in the gospel era, so women's meetings must be lawful. Answer: the text doesn't actually say the church's women met alone to pray. It says Paul went to a riverside where prayer was customarily made and spoke to the women gathered there — most likely a place where Jewish law was read and prayer was customary practice, not a church prayer meeting at all. And critically, there was no established Christian church there yet, so this can't be a model for how church women should behave. None of the women there were even believers yet — Lydia herself hadn't yet come to faith when Paul arrived. It doesn't make sense to treat a gathering of not-yet-believers as the pattern for Christian worship. Paul went to preach to them out of God's grace, the same way he went to the synagogue elsewhere — not because the meeting itself was some special model to imitate. And oddly, if this meeting were really so significant, why did only one of those women — Lydia, and she only a Jewish convert already, not yet a Christian — actually end up converted? That undercuts the idea that this was some spiritually powerful pattern worth copying.

Objection 5 — Mr. K. cites Malachi 3:16. Answer: it doesn't say what he needs it to say — he's even had to swap a word in the verse to make it fit his argument. However he tweaks it, that text simply doesn't establish a duty for women to hold separate worship meetings.

Objection 6 — Jude 20's call to build one another up in faith supports these meetings, unless women aren't meant to grow in faith at all. Answer: the man is grasping at straws. Jude is addressing the whole church, men and women together — not instituting women's meetings. And in any case, the verse says "build up yourselves," which any woman can do through Scripture, private prayer, and meditation, without needing a special meeting for it. This text has nothing to do with women's meetings; reading it that way is stretching Scripture past its actual meaning.

%% section: picking-bones
%% number: 
%% title: Picking the Bones of the Arguments

Now let me go through his five arguments directly. First: he says the Spirit that inspired Miriam is in all believers equally, so any woman can do what Miriam did. But the real question is whether Miriam acted as an ordinary believer or specifically as a prophetess — and it was the latter; she didn't lead women away from the men to worship privately either way. Second: he claims God promised his Spirit specifically so women could pray apart from men. But the prophet he cites never says that — this comes dangerously close to blaming God for causing division in worship. Third: he says God approved of the Acts 16 meeting enough to reveal Christ there. But if unbelievers' actions could earn God's favor like that, that's basically the Catholic doctrine of works-based merit — and besides, only Lydia among those women even feared God at that point. Fourth: he credits Esther's deliverance of the Jews to her and her maids' prayers alone. But didn't Mordecai and the other men fast and pray too? Crediting the whole outcome to the women's prayers, when the text doesn't say that, flatters that sex in a way that could do real harm. Fifth: he appeals directly to the women, asking whether they haven't personally experienced answered prayer through these meetings. This is the worst part of his whole paper — it's silly to make people judges of their own case, and it's actively dangerous, flattering them toward pride. I've seen this pattern before: something good happens, and a small women's prayer group convinces itself it alone brought that blessing down from heaven, when maybe ten thousand others across the land were praying for the same thing just as hard. These women deserve sympathy, not blame — it's Mr. K. and people like him who are leading them astray.

%% section: further-objections
%% number: 
%% title: Three More Objections Answered

Objection 1 — "Where two or three gather in my name, I am there among them" (Matthew 18:20). Answer: gathering "in Christ's name" means gathering under his actual authority and command. There's no command anywhere for women to separate from the men and form their own worship meetings, so these meetings simply aren't "in his name" — and shouldn't happen. Objection 2 — can't women visit a sick friend and pray together before leaving? Answer: sure, but that's different — Scripture's actual instruction for sickness is to call the church elders to pray (James 5:14–15), and that comes with real promises attached. Best to stick with what Scripture actually prescribes. Objection 3 — what about matters too private or embarrassing to bring to men? Answer: a wife and husband are one flesh — tell him. Rachel took her concern to her husband, not to a gathering of women (Genesis 30:1). Or go to God privately in your own room, as Rebekah did, or pray silently in your heart during public worship, as Hannah did — and if the request is a right one, I don't doubt it'll be heard.

%% section: cautionary-conclusion
%% number: 
%% title: A Cautionary Conclusion

Don't let something's respectable-sounding name make you assume it's biblically sound — always check the actual foundation in Scripture. Anything without that foundation is a human invention, however impressive it looks. Monks, nuns, and their vows of celibacy probably started with good intentions too, but without scriptural grounding, none of it carried real holiness. Even something as inherently good as prayer itself can be practiced in a disordered way, and it's fair to question that without being accused of opposing prayer itself. I'm not against prayer — far from it. But I find no real scriptural footing for this particular practice, and I'm convinced Mr. K.'s opinion here is a stranger to Scripture, dressed up to look respectable. As someone responsible for guarding sound doctrine in the church, I've called this practice into question and I intend to keep questioning it until someone shows real scriptural authority for it among God's people here.

None of this is meant to suggest women are somehow lesser members of Christ's body. Spiritually, in Christ, there's no superiority at all — man and woman need each other, and gender doesn't determine standing before God (1 Corinthians 11:11, Galatians 3:28). But that spiritual equality describes the true, invisible church, not necessarily how any one visible congregation organizes itself day to day. A working, orderly congregation needs structure — roles, offices, distinctions — and Paul excludes ordinary women from certain leadership functions within that structure. That's not an insult; it's just how God designed church order to work. And this earthly arrangement won't last forever, dear sisters — when Christ returns, these distinctions fall away completely. In the meantime, you'll share fully in salvation and eternal joy, so long as you continue in faith, love, holiness, and self-control (1 Timothy 2:15).

%% section: to-good-women
%% number: 
%% title: To the Good Women in the Churches

First, honorable women, without real scriptural authority behind them, these meetings amount to worship of your own invention rather than God's command — and that matters when it's measured against God's actual law. Second, they're unnecessary: every church has elders and brethren able to lead worship, if you simply remain within the order you've already been called to. Third, rather than being a credit to your church, these meetings actually look bad — they suggest either that you're acting outside proper order, or that the church isn't managing things well. Fourth, even granting these meetings might sometimes be technically permissible, the men are simply better positioned to lead: they're the ones typically given the relevant gifts, the ones Scripture actually names when commanding prayer meetings, and the ones permitted to lead worship with "open face" before God (1 Corinthians 11:4–5). Given that, deferring to their leadership makes more sense than running parallel meetings of your own.

Fifth, think of women's role in the church a bit like angels in heaven — a real honor, but with a certain deference built in, symbolized by covering the face or head in worship (1 Corinthians 11:10). Angels aren't Christ and don't speak directly to God at the mercy seat; likewise, women aren't positioned to be the public voice of prayer the way men are. I genuinely believe, dear sisters, you can wear this without resentment — the underlying reason traces back to Eve and the fall, which is why women carry certain marks of that position in worship: quietness, and modesty, whenever the church gathers. None of this is a knock against you — modesty and humility make anyone more admirable, in God's eyes and everyone else's. Sixth, none of what I've argued applies to genuinely extraordinary women like Miriam, Deborah, Huldah, or others given prophetic gifts and explicit calling by God — they could teach and prophesy with real authority. But even they still carried outward signs of deference to male leadership (1 Corinthians 11:5). If any of those extraordinary women had explicitly commanded, in God's name, that women should meet separately for worship, I'd have no objection to our women following that example. But nothing like that exists in Scripture — this whole practice originates with one overconfident man's opinion, which is exactly why I felt compelled to answer it.

But let me close with a word directly to the good women in our churches. Pray in private as much as you like — the more, the better. That's genuinely your calling and your privilege, and it doesn't step on anyone's toes or push the men aside. Come to public worship as often as you can, and when you're there, join fully, heart and soul, in the prayers being offered, even though the men are voicing them aloud. Dress and carry yourselves modestly, in keeping with what I've said above. When everyone stays within their calling — women included, but just as much the men — the church as a whole will do better (Jeremiah 29:11–13). I don't fault women for wanting to pray; I just want them to understand the proper bounds. And honestly, I suspect some of this comes from men's own laziness — plenty of men can't be bothered to spend real time with God privately themselves, morning or night, and I include myself in that concern; I need to look at my own house first. There are a few more things about women I may address another time, but for now, I ask that this be received in the spirit it's meant — I'm after building up the church, not starting a fight. THE END.
